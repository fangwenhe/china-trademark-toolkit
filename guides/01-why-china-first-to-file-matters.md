# Guide 01 — Why China's "First–to–File" Rule Matters (and how brands get hijacked)

## 1. The core rule

China grants trademark rights primarily to **the party that files first**, not the party that used the mark first. This is called **first–to–file (注册在先 / 申请在先原则)**.

| | China (first–to–file) | USA (first–to–use) | EU (register + genuine use to keep) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Right arises from | Filing date | Use of the mark | Registration |
| Prior user wins? | Usually **no** | Yes, if use is proven | Only within limits |
| "We've sold here for years" is enough? | **No** | Helpful | Must keep using |

Practical consequence: a Chinese entity that files your brand name **before you do** can become the registered owner in China, even if you created and used the brand worldwide for years.

## 2. How trademark squatting actually happens

Common bad-faith patterns foreign brands meet:

1. **Your distributor / OEM factory / agent registers your mark** in China before you enter the market.
2. A **professional squatter** mass-files known foreign brands not yet registered in China, then waits to sell the mark back or to block you.
3. A **competitor** files your name in your core class or in related classes.
4. Someone files the **Chinese transliteration** of your brand (e.g. a phonetic or a chosen Chinese name) so they own what Chinese customers actually search.
5. Filing in **Class 35** (retail/agency/e-commerce services) or related classes to block your Tmall / Douyin store opening.

## 3. What it costs you if you are too late

- You may be **unable to open an official Tmall / JD / Douyin / Pinduoduo store** (platforms require a Chinese trademark registration or an authorized filing).
- Your goods can be **reported/taken down** or even detained at Chinese customs by the "registered owner".
- You cannot sue infringers in China without standing; **you may instead be the infringer** in China.
- Forced rebrand for the China market, or an expensive buy-back / negotiation.
- Recovery routes (opposition, invalidation, bad-faith cancellation) exist but take **months to years**, require evidence, and are never guaranteed. The "well-known mark" exception is narrow, hard to prove and costly — do not rely on it.

## 4. Defensive strategy (do this before you need it)

1. **File early** — ideally before any China sourcing, trade-show, distributor talk, or marketplace entry.
2. **Cover the right classes**: your products/services **plus** Class 35 and the realistic related/expansion classes. China uses per-class filing; a gap is an opening.
3. **Register both the Latin mark and a Chinese name** (a chosen transliteration/translation), because Chinese consumers search and speak the Chinese version.
4. **Clear the mark first**: run Chinese-character and pinyin similarity searches, not just exact-match.
5. **Set up monitoring** of the CNIPA gazette for identical/similar filings so you can oppose within the 3-month window.
6. Lock down matching **.cn domain and social handles** at the same time.
7. Keep dated **evidence of use and fame** (sales, ads, media, China-related business) — it strengthens any later dispute.

## 5. 60-second urgency check

- We will source from / sell into / exhibit in China within 12 months → **file now**.
- We already have a Chinese distributor or factory → **file now, before they can**.
- Our brand has no Chinese registration and no Chinese name chosen → both are gaps.
- We found an identical/similar pending application in publication → you may have only **weeks** to oppose.

> Bottom line: in China a trademark is a cheap, fast insurance policy (official fee ≈ CNY 270/class). Waiting is the expensive option.
